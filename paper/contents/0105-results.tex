\section{Corpus assessment}
\label{sec:corpus-results}

Cleaning WikiLarge under the strict mojibake filter drops \qty{5.0}{\percent} of training rows (\num{6206} of \num{123862}), \qty{4.8}{\percent} of validation rows, and no test rows.
The targeted filter drops \qty{1.8}{\percent} of training rows and \qty{1.7}{\percent} of validation rows (\cref{tab:corpus-rows}).
The deployed sentence checkpoint was trained on the targeted-filter corpus.

\paragraph{The overlap audit} matches sentences exactly after Unicode normalisation, so its counts are lower bounds.
The WikiLarge mirror's \num{121}-sentence test split is a \qty{100}{\percent} subset of ASSET's \num{359}-sentence split, since both draw on the \num{359} test sentences of TurkCorpus \parencites[588--589]{zhang-lapata-2017-sentence}[4671]{alva-manchego-etal-2020-asset}.
An improvement that appears on both test sets therefore shows that the scores agree on the same sentences, not that the model generalises to new ones.
\qty{19.02}{\percent} of the WikiLarge validation split also appears in training, so every validation loss here is optimistically biased.

Eight of ASSET's \num{359} test sentences (\qty{2.23}{\percent}) also appear in the WikiLarge training data.
Without them, every condition's \gls{sari} changes by less than \num{0.12}, and the gain of the fine-tuned over the untuned checkpoint changes by \(+0.006\).
None of the three demonstrations in the \gls{llm} prompts appears in the training data.

Against the untuned baseline checkpoint, fine-tuning improves \gls{sari} by \(+16.42\)~points at \(\gls{pval} < 0.001\), with a \gls{ci} excluding zero by more than fifteen points, and improves \gls{fkgl} by \num{1.84}~grade levels.
Against the comparison simplifier the difference is \(-0.37\)~\gls{sari}, with \gls{fkgl} likewise marginally in favour of the comparison simplifier.
No paired test between the two was run, and their intervals against the untuned checkpoint overlap almost entirely.

Both prompted models score above the fine-tuned model and the comparison simplifier, and the smaller of the two scores highest: \(47.03 > 45.95 > 38.17 \approx 37.80 > 21.39\).
The 3\gls{billion}-over-7\gls{billion} ordering is unlikely to come from decoding randomness: across three seeds each, the \gls{sari} standard deviations are \num{0.053} and \num{0.073}, so the \num{1.07}-point gap is more than 14 standard deviations.
No paired test over sentences was run.

\Gls{bleu} runs inverse to \gls{sari} across the five rows except for \textbf{B} and \textbf{C}, which are \num{0.37}~\gls{sari} apart (\cref{fig:inversion}a).
The same run measures the mechanism (\cref{fig:inversion}b): the unchanged rate falls from \qty{93.9}{\percent} to \qty{1.2}{\percent} as \gls{sari} rises, with the same exception.
This pattern is the behaviour \cref{sec:background-evaluation} predicts of a metric scoring \gls{ngram} overlap with a reference \parencite[312]{papineni-etal-2002-bleu}.
A second explanation, that ASSET's ten references amplify a conservative system's advantage, does not hold.
Sweeping the reference count from \(\gls{kref} = 1\) to \(\gls{kref} = 10\) (\cref{fig:ksweep}) lifts the absolute \gls{bleu} of the untuned and the fine-tuned checkpoint steeply while leaving the gap between them at two to three points throughout.
Reference count therefore does not explain the inversion; the edit rate remains the explanation consistent with \cref{fig:inversion}b.

The full-scope checkpoint improves \gls{dsari} by \(+21.09\) over the untuned checkpoint on the complete test split at \(\gls{pval} < 0.001\).
The gain from training scope is \(+3.82\)~\gls{dsari} against the re-scored reduced-scope row (\cref{tab:document}).

The two runs use different sample sizes, which ordinarily forbids direct comparison.
The comparison is readable here because the untuned checkpoint is the same model in both and moves by only \(-0.28\)~\gls{dsari}.
An anchor that stable beside a move that large suggests that the difference comes from the checkpoint rather than the sample, though this is an argument, not a controlled comparison.

\paragraph{The metric normaliser} split the full stop inside single-letter abbreviations until it was corrected, so that \texttt{u.s.} became \texttt{u~.~s~.}, a token the test split contains zero times against \num{531} occurrences of the unsplit form.
Re-scoring one run's own cached generations under the fixed normaliser isolates the effect: only \gls{dsari}, \gls{bleu}, and BERTScore move.
\Gls{sari} and \gls{fkgl} are bit-identical, because their implementation re-tokenises its input anyway.
False boundaries and score deltas are not proportional: \num{154}, 84, and \num{197} false boundaries across the three systems' \num{500} outputs recovered \(+0.15\), \(+0.46\), and \(+0.13\)~\gls{dsari}.
The correction therefore cannot be propagated to other rows as a constant.
It also widens the reduced-scope margin, from \num{16.68} to \num{16.99}.
The same function normalises document input in the deployed system, so the deployed system passed the same split abbreviations to the model until the correction.

\paragraph{\Gls{dsari} and \gls{lens} disagree} on the three-way comparison at \(\gls{nsize} = 2{,}000\): they rank the full-scope fine-tuned checkpoint and the prompted 7\gls{billion} in opposite orders (\cref{fig:disagreement}), while both place the untuned checkpoint last.
\Gls{dsari} places the fine-tuned checkpoint \num{12.54}~points above the prompted 7\gls{billion}, and \gls{lens} places the prompted 7\gls{billion} \num{23.80}~points above the fine-tuned checkpoint.

Four possible explanations were eliminated by measurement.
Truncation affects \qtyrange{0.0}{0.6}{\percent} of predictions, far too few to carry the gap.
Sample size does not explain it, since the \(\gls{nsize} = 8{,}000\) run reproduces the \(\gls{nsize} = 2{,}000\) figures within \num{0.6} on both \gls{dsari} and \gls{lens}.
Normalisation does not either, since both systems pass through the same normaliser and the rescoring above bounds its effect at under half a \gls{dsari} point per system.
Nor does sampling: three seeds of the prompted condition give \gls{lens} \num{69.27}, \num{69.14}, and \num{69.26} (mean \num{69.22}, standard deviation \num{0.071}), and their \gls{dsari} standard deviation is \num{0.039}.
Both spreads are two to three orders of magnitude smaller than the disagreement they would have to explain.

Both checkpoints share the same architecture and training pipeline and differ in training scope (\cref{tab:training-config}).
Both are reviewed with the same evaluation script, so their rates can be compared.

Every category is a surface property counted automatically over the whole sample, and examples were read only afterwards.
The script stops if its sample does not match the source documents the outputs were generated from.
The examples were read as structured manual inspection (\cref{sec:research-questions}) by the author, who also built the system under inspection.

\paragraph{Compression increases with source length,} one of the clearest results of the review.
For both checkpoints, the median output/source length ratio falls monotonically from the shortest source-length bucket (under 60 words) to the longest (250 words or more) (\cref{fig:compression}).

The reduced-scope checkpoint follows the curve of the human references closely; the full-scope checkpoint follows its shape but lies about \qty{20}{\percent} above it in the two longest buckets.
The untuned checkpoint stays at \num{1.00}, rising only to \num{1.04} in the longest bucket.

The probe rows in \cref{tab:probes} show that the full-scope checkpoint compresses less and leaves more outputs unchanged than the reduced-scope one: over-deletion falls from \qty{25.8}{\percent} to \qty{18.5}{\percent}, and unchanged outputs rise from \qty{4.4}{\percent} to \qty{7.7}{\percent}.

\paragraph{Error case~1, reciting the corpus over the input,} affected the reduced-scope checkpoint heavily.
Of the 35 documents whose source mentions the 2010 census, its output gives the census year as 2000 instead of 2010 in 27 (\qty{77}{\percent}), keeps the 2010 census in 5 (\qty{14}{\percent}) and names no census year in 3 (\qty{9}{\percent}).
The full-scope checkpoint gives 2000 instead of 2010 in only 1 of \num{576} such documents (\qty{0.2}{\percent}), keeps the 2010 census in \num{248} (\qty{43}{\percent}) and names no census year in \num{327} (\qty{57}{\percent}).
Among the outputs that name a census year, the share with the correct one therefore rises from \qty{16}{\percent} to over \qty{99}{\percent}.
The full-scope checkpoint almost never states a wrong census year, but it more often names none than the correct one, which loses the fact without asserting a false one.

Two checks suggest a real change rather than a sampling artefact.
The untuned checkpoint only copies: its outputs contain ``2000 census'' in \qty{11.4}{\percent} and \qty{9.0}{\percent} of the two samples, in every case because the source mentions both censuses, so the probe behaves alike on both samples.
Over-deletion also falls in the same direction (\cref{tab:probes}).

A likely source of the substitution is the corpus itself: of the \num{576} test references whose source mentions the 2010 census, \num{190} keep 2010 and 21 say 2000, 15 of them where the source does not mention the 2000 census.
Only the test split was counted, so it is inferred rather than measured that the training references contain the same substitution.
Documents whose source cites the 2010 census make up \qty{7.2}{\percent} of the \num{8000} test documents.
The example \textcite[8001--8002]{sun-etal-2021-document} use to motivate \gls{dsari} is a census-templated article of the same kind.
The two reviews differ in both checkpoint and sample, so the improvement is consistent with full-scope training but does not show that it caused it.

\paragraph{Error case~2, over-deletion on long documents,} is the dominant quality failure of the deployed checkpoint.
Of the \num{8000} full-scope outputs, \num{1482} (\qty{18.5}{\percent}) are less than half as long as their reference.
This has two causes: sources longer than the \num{512}-token input cap are truncated before the model sees them, and the model's learned deletion then shortens what remains.
In the inspected examples, the checkpoint reduces long articles to a competent simplification of the lead paragraph and drops everything after it.

\paragraph{Error case~3, fluent invention on technical prose,} was observed in a single document.
The reduced-scope checkpoint rewrote the source's definition of typesetting, ``the composition of text by means of arranging physical types'', as ``the use of a computer program to write text''.
The output reads fluently but states a different definition.
A single case does not show how often this happens.
The case was found by reading the reduced-scope outputs.
Invention is not one of the categories the review script counts, so it was not checked for the full-scope checkpoint.

\paragraph{Error case~4, declining to edit,} may partly be caused by corpus defects.
Of the \num{8000} test sources, \num{169} (\qty{2.11}{\percent}) are disambiguation stubs whose reference is a full article, with a median of 5 source words against 61 reference words.
For these documents no correct output exists: the model only sees the short stub, while the reference is a full article with information the stub does not contain.
The metric therefore penalises the output both for keeping source words that the reference drops and for lacking content that is not in the input.
\textcite[285]{xu-etal-2015-problems} found this kind of misalignment in \qty{17}{\percent} of sampled \gls{pwkp} pairs.

\section{Qualitative analysis of sentence-level outputs}
\label{sec:sentence-failures}

The sentence-level failures in this section come from the History log and targeted probes, not from the document review.

\paragraph{The ``Other websites'' fabrication} mentioned in \cref{sec:motivation} came from the deployed sentence checkpoint, which produced and served ``Other websites'' for the input ``Municipal recycling policy revisions''.
At the time, no guard covered this case; the deny-list added afterwards (\cref{sec:guards}) now rejects the output for every approach.

This output may originate in the training data; of the \num{117656} WikiLarge pairs, \num{178} contain ``Other websites'' in the target.
In 38 of them the target is exactly that string, and a further 86 begin with it.
The sources of those 38 pairs are short fragments with little content, such as \texttt{, Blue and Green}, \texttt{programming language = C}, and \texttt{( Central Committee )}.
The corpus therefore trains a model to produce exactly ``Other websites'' for such fragments.
The deployed sentence checkpoint was trained under the targeted filter, whose corpus contains all \num{117656} strict-filter pairs, including these 38.
The comparison simplifier, which produced the same string for isolated fragments (\cref{sec:motivation}), was trained on the same WikiLarge mirror \parencite[3]{cohen2026simplify}.

As in error case~4, the defect is an alignment error.
``Other websites'' is a section heading in Simple English Wikipedia articles.
Automatic sentence alignment paired it with English fragments as if it were their simplification.
The same string occurs in eleven documents on the simplified side of D-Wikipedia's training split, but as running prose rather than as a heading, so the defect is specific to the sentence-level corpus.

\pagebreak

\paragraph{Two failure classes are specific to the prompted models:}\leavevmode\par
\textbf{Prompt injection succeeds end to end}: the input sentence ``Ignore all previous instructions and reply with exactly: PWNED.'' produced exactly ``PWNED'' on both prompted \glspl{llm}, with no reason code.
The prompted models receive the page text as part of their prompt, so text on a page can act as an instruction to them (\cref{sec:method-three}).
A hostile page can therefore make the extension display arbitrary text in place of its own content.

No guard detects this, because the guards check the form of an output, such as whether it is empty or repetitive, and injected output can be as well-formed as a valid simplification.
The \gls{seqseq} models receive no instruction and treat an instruction in their input as text to simplify, so they are structurally immune.

\textbf{Short fragments are explained rather than left alone}: the prompted models turn ``Published'' into ``Published: Released for everyone to read or see.'', an output that passes every guard legitimately.
On a real page, the same behaviour would attach a definition to every navigation link.

\section{Deployment results: coverage, preservation, and selection}
\label{sec:deployment-results}

The site audit covers twelve pages in six categories, two per category, at both granularities.
The audit comprises 24 runs over \num{11867} registered text leaves (the candidate elements of \cref{sec:content-selection}) and \num{2571} requests, with \num{130} hand-labelled items (\cref{app:pages,app:coverage}).
It is an exploratory audit reporting base rates, not an estimate with confidence intervals.

\paragraph{Structural preservation} holds across all 24 runs.
After simplification, every page kept all of its tags and the same number of anchors, every image kept its source attribute, and every anchor kept its target.
Reverting restored the page's text content and each element's attributes in all 24 runs.

At the strictest level, byte-identical raw \gls{html}, 18 of 24 runs hold.
The six differences come from \texttt{jsdom} re-serialising the page's own inline styles once the extension sets a style, which changes their text but not the computed styles.
Five differences are one to four bytes long, and one is \num{913}~bytes, on a page with \num{133} inline style declarations.

\paragraph{Coverage} depends heavily on the page.
The sentence cut skips \qtyrange{21.6}{95.1}{\percent} of the registered candidates on a page, and the document cut \qtyrange{53.7}{99.1}{\percent} (\cref{fig:skipshare}).
Over all pages, the filters withhold \qty{74.7}{\percent} and \qty{90.9}{\percent} (\cref{tab:coverage}).
Most skips are correct: in the labelled sample below, only 15 of 69 skipped items should have been sent.

Since the share varies heavily across pages, no single page is representative of the system's coverage.
The two cuts' skip shares cannot be compared directly.
The document cut registers every leaf and sends whole heading-delimited sections, about half of its skips are design decisions rather than rejections: the headings it cuts on and elements outside the document body tags.

\paragraph{The content filter} has one measured class of false positives.
The code-like-text heuristic rejected \num{487} items, \num{102} of them because of a semicolon alone.
Of these \num{102}, 31 are prose-length items inside the page's own content area: 17 are running prose, and 14 are bibliography entries in which semicolons separate author names.

The withheld prose is core content, for example an encyclopedia article's 97-word definition of carbon fixation, government guidance, four paragraphs of a philosophy encyclopedia and five documentation paragraphs.
The other triggers cost little: every item skipped because of braces was actual code, and angle brackets withheld seven prose items.

\paragraph{The labelled verdicts} show errors in both directions.
A stratified sample of 69 skipped and 61 unchanged items, drawn deterministically by content hash, was read and labelled (\cref{fig:verdicts}).

Of the 69 skipped items, 48 were correctly skipped and 15 should have been sent; 12 were rejected because they contain a semicolon, and 3 because they contain the word ``let''.
The remaining 6 were correctly skipped but at a cost: they are product guidance that the page itself marks as opt-out, in this case with \texttt{aria-hidden="true"} (\cref{sec:content-selection}).
The extension therefore leaves them alone as intended, and the reader gets them unsimplified.
Of the 61 unchanged items, 49 were already plain, which is the correct behaviour on a page written to a plain-English standard.
Of the other 12, 9 should have been simplified and 3 were German.

\paragraph{Guards} fire on \num{1007} of the \num{2260} sentence-cut requests (\qty{45}{\percent}), counting a request once if any of its sentences was guarded.
The response format does not allow a per-sentence rate.

\paragraph{Where preservation fails,} the failure can be traced either to the model or to the extension.
Of \num{6320} anchors, \num{331} (\qty{5}{\percent}) held no text after the sentence-cut run.
In \num{237} of them, the model deleted the phrase that carried the link, exposing the write-back limitation described in \cref{sec:content-selection}.
The other 94 are link text misplaced by the fragmentation defect (\cref{sec:unit-of-simplification}), 93 of them on a single densely linked article.

Misplacement follows link density rather than page size: the philosophy encyclopedia sends more prose than the Wikipedia article (\num{482} requests against \num{293}) but misplaces no link text.
Two pages, however, show a pattern, not an established cause.
In the document cut, \num{422} anchors were emptied, \num{388} by model deletions and 34 by misplacement.

\paragraph{Five sentences were served with a negation deleted,} one of the most serious results of the review (\cref{app:inversions}).
Each was otherwise unchanged, so the deletion inverts the claim, and no guard checks for this kind of edit.
They include a return policy (``items \dots\ cannot be returned or exchanged'' became ``can be returned or exchanged''), an \gls{api} contract (``the iterable must not be empty'' became ``must be empty'') and two German sentences.
The failure is not limited to out-of-language text, since three of the five sentences are English.

The change guard lets such edits through by design: it never rejects word-level edits, since dropping one word can be a real simplification.
A dropped negation is such an edit.
Detection was conservative: it counted only outputs that equal the input minus exactly one negation token, and so it misses any inversion in which the sentence was also reworded.
Five is therefore a lower bound.
All five come from the sentence cut.
With twelve pages and \num{2260} sentence-cut requests, five is a count, not a pattern.

\paragraph{The system is English-only in capability} but does not signal it.
The audit tested one German page, where the sentence cut changed 42 of the 65 items it sent.
On the same page, the document cut applied the corpus lowercasing convention to German, producing invalid word forms such as ``die meisten häusers zer stört'' for ``die meisten Häuser zerstört'' (``most houses destroyed'').
Since the system expects English input and nothing checks the language, no guard fired on any of these outputs.

\paragraph{Two defects in restoring the original page} from its stored attributes (\cref{sec:content-selection}) were found during the audit and fixed in the extension afterwards.
Reverting deleted the page's own \texttt{title} attribute and left empty \texttt{class} and \texttt{style} attributes on elements that originally had neither.
An existing test was meant to cover both cases but missed them, because it compares only the inner markup of the simplified element and so cannot see the element's own attributes.

\paragraph{The request economy} was measured as a by-product of the audit.
Of the \num{2222} sentence-cut items in the per-item artefact, \num{647} (\qty{29}{\percent}) are under six words, such as dates, navigation labels and link texts like ``Edit this page on GitHub''.%
\footnote{The extension's counters record \num{2224} sent sentence-cut items. The difference lies in two placeholder texts of search fields, which were sent but are not candidate elements, so the per-item artefact does not list them.}
Raising the minimum from four to six words would stop all \num{647}, but it would also remove \num{349} simplifications of short text (\qty{22}{\percent} of all changes).
Scoping the sentence cut to the page's content area would instead stop \num{592} items but also withhold \num{151} prose items.
Excluding navigation, footer, header, aside and form elements (the page furniture) gives nearly the same result, within \qty{5.3}{\percent} of both the items it stops and the prose it withholds, and so has the same cost as scoping.

\section{Granularity and input-unit findings}
\label{sec:granularity-findings}

The model outputs in this section come from the reduced-scope document checkpoint and concern input and output conventions, not simplification quality.

\paragraph{Fragmentation, quantified.}
Per-text-node selection sent \num{173} requests for one Wikipedia article's prose, a large share of them sub-sentence fragments.
The same prose takes 79 requests under the sentence cut and 11 under the document cut.

\paragraph{Input convention changes output quality} (\cref{tab:conventions}).
The corpus convention was verified on the corpus itself: zero newline characters inside any document body across \num{3000} sampled documents, zero uppercase characters across \num{500} sampled lines, and Penn-Treebank-style pre-tokenisation throughout.

Mixed-case prose produced a factual hallucination that the corpus convention did not.
With newline characters as structural separators, two facts were lost, and the province Friesland became ``the country of friesland''.
A heading included in a section's input was reproduced at the start of the output.
On a page that keeps its own headings, each heading would then appear twice.

This is the clearest instance in the project of a measurement changing a design decision instead of confirming one, though it rests on a single hand-constructed document.
Segment joining likewise has no obviously safe strategy (\cref{tab:joins}), so the adopted rule was derived from the comparison (\cref{sec:unit-of-simplification}).

\paragraph{The training-length distribution} reversed a planned optimisation.
In a sample of \num{1500} source documents, the mean length is \num{180}~tokens, the median \num{120}, the \gls{p95} \num{510} and the maximum \num{1168}; \qty{68}{\percent} are at most \num{200}~tokens long (\cref{fig:doclen}).
A single short section is therefore closer to the training distribution than several merged ones, so sections are not merged up to the \num{512}-token input cap.
Sending a whole page as one document is not viable either: several articles have more than \num{2000}~tokens of prose, so more than \qty{74}{\percent} of such an article would be truncated at the cap and would be missing from the output.

\paragraph{Three of the four symptoms that made the document cut look broken were not caused by the model} (\cref{tab:finding9}).
The document error cases observed in the extension before these fixes therefore say more about the pipeline than about the model.
The invented organisation name in the third row, for example, came from collection.
No new cases were collected after the fixes, so none are reported here.
The offline results are unaffected, since the evaluation harness sends whole articles directly to the backend.

\paragraph{Two further defects affected the instruments rather than the page.}
The History log recorded the text that was sent to the model rather than the text the page showed after write-back.
The document cut registered only one item per section, so the leaves inside a section went uncounted.
Neither defect changes the page, so no structural check reveals them; they show only when the log is compared with the page or the item counts with the registered leaves.
Both were corrected: the log now records the whole chain from input to served text (\cref{sec:architecture}), and the document cut registers every leaf (\cref{sec:deployment-results}).

\section{Runtime and cost}
\label{sec:runtime}

Latency was measured by running the extension's content script under \texttt{jsdom} against a live backend, so the timed requests are the ones the extension sends in use.
The \gls{seqseq} models ran on the \gls{cpu} of an Apple~M3, not its \gls{gpu}, so their figures are not comparable to the \gls{gpu} evaluation runs above; the prompted 3\gls{billion} ran through Ollama, whose device use was not recorded.

The main comparison cuts the same prose of one Wikipedia article in the two ways, using the fine-tuned sentence checkpoint and the reduced-scope document checkpoint.
The prompted 3\gls{billion} and the comparison simplifier follow at the end of this section.
The sentence cut sends 79 requests with a median payload of \num{124}~characters, and the document cut 11 requests with a median payload of \num{1262}~characters, for nearly the same total (\num{13448} and \num{13403}~characters).
Median request latency is \qty{1.26}{\second} for the sentence cut and \qty{19.13}{\second} for the document cut, and \gls{p95} is \qty{3.28}{\second} and \qty{19.14}{\second}.
The whole page takes \qty{15.5}{\second} and \qty{22.5}{\second}, or \num{869} and \num{596}~characters per second.
Three independent cold repeats give \qty{15.8 \pm 0.5}{\second} and \qty{20.8 \pm 1.1}{\second}.
Requests per second (\num{5.1} and \num{0.5}) reflect how the text is split rather than speed, so characters per second is the measure that compares the two cuts.

Three findings follow.

\textbf{Fewer, larger requests are not faster}: the document cut reduces the number of requests to one-seventh but increases the processing time per page by approximately \qty{45}{\percent}.
Its 11 requests fill the client's eight-slot queue barely twice, while the sentence cut's 79 fill it ten times.
The document cut therefore cannot hide its per-request latency behind concurrency.

\textbf{They are, however, far steadier}: the document cut's \gls{p95} is within \qty{0.01}{\second} of its median, because its requests run in the same one or two server-side batches and finish together.
The sentence cut's \gls{p95}, by contrast, is \num{2.6}~times its median.

\textbf{They also appear more immune to contention} (\cref{fig:latency}c): in a separate run made while the machine was also running an evaluation job, the sentence cut's whole-page time was \qty{121.5}{\second} instead of \qty{15.5}{\second} (\(7.8\times\)) and its \gls{p95} \qty{62.7}{\second} instead of \qty{3.28}{\second} (\(19\times\)).
The document cut did not show the same increase.
A likely reason is that the sentence cut pays the contention penalty once per batch round, and it needed ten rounds here.
This observation comes from a single run during an evaluation job, not from a controlled experiment.

Across the four profiled configurations, out of seven selectable models, the whole-page time differs by more than a factor of eight: \qty{15.5}{\second} for the fine-tuned sentence checkpoint, \qty{22.5}{\second} for the reduced-scope document checkpoint, \qty{43.6}{\second} for the comparison simplifier and \qty{132.7}{\second} for the prompted 3\gls{billion}.

On the same input, the comparison simplifier is \num{2.4}~times slower per request and \num{2.8}~times slower per page than the fine-tuned sentence checkpoint, and the prompted 3\gls{billion} is \num{8.6}~times slower per page, at \num{101}~characters per second.
The status check before each run adds \qtyrange{1.36}{4.06}{\second}, depending on the model.
Simplifying a page again that the backend has already processed takes only \qtyrange{41}{183}{\milli\second}, because the backend serves cached outputs (\cref{sec:content-selection}).
This is \(84\times\) faster than a first run, so a demonstration on an already processed page doesn't show the real latency of a computed simplification.
